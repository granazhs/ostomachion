What you are looking at

The Ostomachion — from the Greek osto (bone) and máchi (battle), literally a “battle of the bones” — is a dissection puzzle attributed to Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BC). Fourteen pieces of 11 different shapes tile a 12×12 square of area 144.

This project enumerates every way the pieces can be reassembled into the square, classifies the solutions, and visualizes them all.

History

The Stomachion, also called the loculus Archimedius (“Archimedes’ box”), is one of the oldest known puzzles. It is described in fragmentary ancient texts and in a treatise attributed to Archimedes. The only surviving copy of the treatise is a single page of the Archimedes Palimpsest — a Byzantine parchment manuscript from the 10th century that was scraped clean in the 13th century so that the pages could be reused for a prayer book. The faint underlying text was recovered in the 20th century, and modern multispectral imaging let scholars read it fully.

The puzzle itself consists of 14 flat pieces — 7 triangles, 5 quadrilaterals and 2 pentagons — whose corners lie on a 12×12 grid. The aim is to rearrange them into a square. Ancient writers (Ausonius and others) compared it to juggling poetic meters, and it was long regarded as a playful pastime.

The puzzle is notable mathematically because two pairs of pieces are congruent triangles (A/B and D/E), so any count of solutions must decide whether swapping them counts as a new solution. Three further pairs (I+L, J+M, K+N) always appear fused together in every tiling of the square — a fact this project proves exhaustively in forced_pairs.md.

How many solutions?

The exact count was first computed in 2003 by Bill Cutler: 17,152 arrangements in total, of which 536 are geometrically distinct once rotations, reflections and swaps of the congruent triangles are identified. The counts here were reproduced independently by this project’s own exact-cover solver.

“Unlabeled” treats the congruent triangles A/B and D/E (and, in the simple game, G/K) as interchangeable and identifies mirror images and rotations. The simple game is exactly Cutler’s reduced 11-piece “Stomach”: half of 536, because the third congruent pair halves the count again.

What we did here

  • Built an exact-cover solver (count_solutions.js) that enumerates every tiling of the 12×12 square, using big-integer arithmetic on the arrangement of all piece edges. It finds all 17152 arrangements in a few minutes and reports the four counting conventions above.
  • Proved the three fused pairs by brute force: in all 1544 solutions of the classic game, the pieces I–L, J–M and K–N always appear together and share an edge.
  • Recorded, for every solution, which pieces are mirror-flipped (a piece is “flipped” when the tiling uses its mirror image), and which maximal cut-lines cross the whole board (spanning cuts).
  • Generated complete visual galleries for both games: every distinct tiling as an SVG, a flip table, and the spanning-cut case analysis.
